About these forecasts

How do I read the chart?

The chart shows the recent observed river height differential (the black line) and a 10-day probabilistic forecast (the blue spaghetti). Each thin blue line is one possible future trajectory driven by a different rainfall ensemble member; the bold purple line is the ensemble mean and is the single most likely outcome. Horizontal bands shade the flag colours, daily rainfall is shown as bars on the right axis, and the dashed red line marks "now". For Isis and Godstow, the stacked chart underneath shows the probability that the river will be in each flag colour at each point in the next 10 days.

What is the shaded band?

The 50 blue lines only show how much the rainfall forecasts disagree. Even with the rain exactly right the model is sometimes wrong, and in the first day or two that is most of the real uncertainty, because the rain forecasts barely disagree yet. The purple shading adds that in. The model was re-run every 6 hours over periods it was never trained on (2023, and November 2024 to January 2026) using the rain that actually fell, and every one of those forecasts' errors was kept. For today's forecast, each rain line borrows the errors of past forecasts that started from a similar situation (similar river level, trend, recent and forecast rain, and time of year). The outer shading holds 80% of the resulting outcomes and the inner shading 50%. The flag probabilities underneath are worked out from the same outcomes, so they include the model's own uncertainty too.

How have earlier forecasts done?

Under each chart you can look back. Past forecast: drag the slider to any forecast from the last few weeks (one is saved every 6 hours) and see what it said against what the river then did, with its typical error and how often it had the flag right. Forecasts of now: what every forecast from the last ten days said the latest reading would be. Forecasts saved before the model-error band was added only show the rain spread, not the model-error band.

How reliable is the summary sentence?

The summary sentence is simply a short description of the forecast, without any human intervention. The certainty bounds are just based off the spread of the forecast. Importantly, the summary sentence is based off the change from the first (+2h) prediction, so it doesn't take into account the initial jump at t=0. This summary sentence is meant to give a general idea of the forecast, which is probably more reliable than the prediction chart itself.

Where does the data come from?

Historical river levels and rainfall come from the Environment Agency's flood-monitoring and hydrology APIs. Rainfall forecasts come from the ECMWF AI Forecasting System (ECMWF-AIFS) via Open-Meteo. The model is physics-based: a small neural network reads the recent past and sets the starting state of a rainfall-runoff model (soil wetness, fast and slow runoff stores, groundwater and the river channel). Forecast rain is routed through those stores hour by hour, and a rating curve that only ever increases turns river flow into the differential. The forecast is the average of five independently trained copies. For the Isis, the network also sees a simulation of the whole catchment run continuously through the last two years of rain (how wet the soil is, how much water is in the groundwater), so it remembers dry summers and wet winters for longer than the last month of data.

Why hasn't the forecast updated recently?

Usually it's just a temporary glitch in an Environment Agency sensor going briefly offline; the page will fall back to the latest successful forecast. If a sensor has been offline for more than four hours the page flags the forecast as stale. Updates are also scheduled hourly via GitHub Actions and sometimes queue up, so refreshes may not land exactly on the hour.

Is the forecast reliable?

Please treat this as experimental. The model starts exactly at the latest observation and cannot rise without rain, but it can overshoot after heavy rain and still misses some sudden weir changes. Issues and improvements get written up on the substack; bug reports and ideas are very welcome on GitHub.
